3. Motion Control ​

3.1 Mecanum Chassis Motion Analysis ​

3.1.1 Hardware Structure ​

A Mecanum wheel consists of a hub and a set of barrel-shaped rollers mounted around it. Each roller axis is angled at 45° to the wheel axis. Mecanum wheels are normally used in sets of four, with two left-handed Type A wheels and two right-handed Type B wheels. Type A and Type B wheels are mirror images of each other.

Four Mecanum wheels can be arranged in several patterns, such as AAAA, BBBB, AABB, ABAB, and BABA. Not every arrangement supports forward and backward motion, rotation, and lateral movement. ClawPi uses the ABAB arrangement, which enables omnidirectional motion.

3.1.2 Physical Characteristics ​

By controlling the direction and speed of each wheel, the individual wheel forces combine into a resultant force vector in the required direction. This allows the chassis to move freely in that direction without steering the wheels themselves.

The angled rollers around the rim allow the wheel to slide laterally. As the wheel rotates about its fixed axle, the outer surfaces of the rollers form a cylindrical envelope, allowing the wheel to roll forward continuously while also generating a lateral force component.

3.1.3 Kinematic Principles and Equations ​

The Mecanum chassis kinematic model uses the following parameters:

Vx: Chassis velocity along the X-axis, normally the forward/backward direction.

Vy: Chassis velocity along the Y-axis, normally the left/right direction.

Vω: Angular velocity of the chassis about its center.

VA,VB,VC,VD: Instantaneous velocities of wheels A, B, C, and D.

💡 Equation Guide: Beginners May Skip This Section

  • Vx: Forward/backward velocity. Forward is positive, and backward is negative.
  • Vy: Lateral velocity. Right is positive, and left is negative.
  • Vω: Angular velocity. Counterclockwise rotation is positive.
  • VA,VB,VC,VD: Rotational speeds of the four wheels.

In simple terms: The speed of each wheel is determined by combining three components: forward/backward motion, lateral motion, and chassis rotation.

Using the front-right wheel as an example, its motion in the plane can be resolved into the following components:

  • VBx: Wheel velocity along the X-axis, normally the forward/backward direction.
  • VBy: Wheel velocity along the Y-axis, normally the left/right direction.
  • L: Distance between the left and right wheel centers, or track width.
  • H: Distance between the front and rear wheel centers, or wheelbase.
  • θω: Angle between the chassis center and the front-right wheel center, which is 45°.

These parameters are used to analyze the kinematics of the Mecanum chassis. The key equations are shown below.

  • Kinematic Equations:

The model uses the following idealized assumptions:

1. The omnidirectional wheels do not slip on the ground, and the ground provides sufficient friction.

2. The four wheels are mounted at the corners of a rectangle or square and remain parallel to one another.

Here, the chassis motion is decomposed into three components: translation along X+, translation along Y+, and rotation about Z+. After calculating the four wheel speeds for each component, the equations can be combined to obtain the wheel speeds required for simultaneous translation and rotation.

Here, VA, VB, VC, and VD are the rotational speeds of wheels A, B, C, and D, which are also the motor speeds. Vx is the chassis translational velocity along the X-axis, Vy is the translational velocity along the Y-axis, and Vω is the rotational velocity about the Z-axis.

12L is half the track width, and 12H is half the wheelbase.

1. When the robot translates along the X-axis, each wheel-speed component is calculated as follows:

VA=+Vx,VB=+Vx,VC=+Vx,VD=+Vx

Here, VA, VB, VC, and VD are the instantaneous wheel velocities, and Vx is the chassis velocity along the X-axis.

2. When the robot translates along the Y-axis, each wheel-speed component is calculated as follows:

VA=−Vy,VB=+Vy,VC=−Vy,VD=+Vy

Here, Vy is the chassis velocity along the Y-axis.

3. When the robot rotates about the Z-axis, each wheel-speed component is calculated as follows:

VA=−Vω∗(12L+12H),VB=Vω∗(12L+12H)

VC=−Vω∗(12L+12H),VD=Vω∗(12L+12H)

Here, Vω is the angular velocity of the chassis about its center.

4. Combining the X-, Y-, and Z-axis velocity components gives the four wheel speeds for the required chassis motion:

VA=Vx−Vy−Vω∗(12L+12H),VB=Vx+Vy+Vω∗(12L+12H)

VC=Vx−Vy−Vω∗(12L+12H),VD=Vx+Vy+Vω∗(12L+12H)

3.3 Publishing IMU and Odometry Data ​

Accurate real-time position estimation is essential for robot navigation.

Odometry is normally calculated from motor encoder data and the robot's kinematic model. However, wheel-based odometry can become inaccurate in situations such as wheel slip or when the robot is lifted and moved without the wheels rotating.

Fusing IMU and wheel odometry data reduces errors caused by wheel slip and accumulated drift, improving mapping and navigation accuracy.

3.3.1 Introduction to IMU and Odometry ​

An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is a device used to measure an object's three-axis attitude, or angular rate, and acceleration. Its main components are a gyroscope and an accelerometer, which together provide six degrees of freedom for measuring angular velocity and acceleration in three-dimensional space.

Odometry is a method that uses data obtained from motion sensors to estimate how an object's position changes over time. It is widely used in robotic systems to estimate how far a robot has moved relative to its initial position.

Common odometry-based localization methods include wheel odometry, visual odometry, and visual-inertial odometry. ClawPi uses wheel odometry.

The principle of wheel odometry can be explained with a simple example. Imagine driving a horse-drawn cart from point A to point B and wanting to determine how far the cart has traveled.

If the wheel circumference is known and a device is installed to count the number of wheel rotations while the cart is moving, the wheel circumference, travel time, and recorded number of rotations can be used to determine the distance from point A to point B.

For a wheeled robot, odometry provides a basic pose estimate, but it has an obvious limitation: error accumulates over time. In addition to unavoidable hardware tolerances, environmental conditions can also introduce error. For example, weather or surface conditions may cause the tires to slip, so the odometry error can increase as the robot travels farther.

Therefore, both the IMU and odometry are important to the robot. The IMU measures the object's three-axis attitude, or angular rate, and acceleration, while odometry estimates the robot's distance and pose relative to its initial position, as well as its speed and direction of travel.

To reduce this error, IMU data is combined with odometry data. Fusing the measurements from both sources provides a more accurate estimate.

IMU data is published on the /imu topic, while odometry data is published on /odom. After both data streams are obtained, the ROS 2 ekf package fuses them and republishes the fused localization information.

(4) Functions

python
def rpy2qua(roll, pitch, yaw):
    cy = math.cos(yaw*0.5)
    sy = math.sin(yaw*0.5)
    cp = math.cos(pitch*0.5)
    sp = math.sin(pitch*0.5)
    cr = math.cos(roll * 0.5)
    sr = math.sin(roll * 0.5)
    
    q = Pose()
    q.orientation.w = cy * cp * cr + sy * sp * sr
    q.orientation.x = cy * cp * sr - sy * sp * cr
    q.orientation.y = sy * cp * sr + cy * sp * cr
    q.orientation.z = sy * cp * cr - cy * sp * sr
    return q.orientation

def qua2rpy(x, y, z, w):
    roll = math.atan2(2 * (w * x + y * z), 1 - 2 * (x * x + y * y))
    pitch = math.asin(2 * (w * y - x * z))
    yaw = math.atan2(2 * (w * z + x * y), 1 - 2 * (z * z + y * y))
  
    return roll, pitch, yaw

The rpy2qua function converts Euler angles, one method of representing rotation, into a quaternion, another method of representing rotation.

💡 What Are Quaternions and Euler Angles? Both are methods of representing rotation:

  • Euler angles use roll, pitch, and yaw. They are intuitive but can suffer from gimbal lock.
  • Quaternions use four values. They are efficient and robust for computation but less intuitive.

In simple terms: Euler angles are like describing a direction using compass points, while quaternions are like specifying it with coordinates. They represent the same orientation in different ways.

The qua2rpy function converts a quaternion to Euler angles.

3.5 Troubleshooting ​

Q1: IMU Calibration Fails

Possible causes: The robot was not positioned in the specified orientation, or it was moved during calibration.

Solutions:

1. Position the robot exactly as shown for each calibration step.

2. Keep the robot stationary and do not touch it during calibration.

3. The initial orientation defines the robot's forward direction. Use this direction as the reference for all subsequent positions.

Q2: How Can a File Be Saved and Vim Closed?

1. Press Esc to leave Insert mode. 2. Enter :wq, and then press Enter to save the file and exit. 3. To exit without saving, enter :q! and press Enter.

Q3: The Robot Does Not Move After a Velocity Command Is Issued

Possible causes: The controller is not running, the commanded velocity is too low, or the battery level is low.

Solutions:

1. Verify that ros2 launch controller controller.launch.py has been run to start the controller. 2. Check the linear velocity setting. A value of at least 0.1 is recommended. 3. Check the battery voltage on the 1.9-inch display screen and charge the battery if necessary.

Q4: The Robot Spins in Place Instead of Traveling Straight

Possible causes: The IMU has not been calibrated, or the angular velocity calibration parameter is incorrect.

Solutions:

1. Calibrate the IMU as described in 3.2.1 IMU Calibration. 2. Calibrate the angular velocity as described in 3.2.2 Angular Velocity Calibration. 3. Restart the controller after calibration, and test the robot again.

Q5: Pressing Ctrl+C Does Not Stop the Program

Possible causes: The program is not handling the interrupt signal correctly, or the terminal is unresponsive.

Solutions:

1. Press Ctrl+C several times. 2. Open a new terminal and run ~/.stop_ros.sh to stop all ROS 2 services. 3. If the program still does not stop, restart the robot.
